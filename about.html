Conservation Mission

Preserving the Heritage of Ceylon Tuskers

The Sri Lankan elephant (Elephas maximus maximus) is the largest of the three recognized Asian elephant subspecies. Among them, tuskers (ඇතුන්) occupy a legendary position in religious tradition, ancient history, and wildlife folklore. Yet their survival in the wild hangs by a delicate thread.

The Genetic Rarity of Sri Lankan Tuskers

In continental Asian elephant populations across India and Thailand, a significant percentage of males possess tusks. In Sri Lanka, however, less than five percent of wild males are tuskers. Centuries of selective pressure, capture for ancient royal stables, and relentless trophy hunting during 19th-century colonial rule systematically wiped out the dominant tusk-bearing bulls.

The Perils Facing Wild Giants

Wild tuskers today navigate a landscape fractured by human encroachment, farmlands, and highways. The principal threats include:

  • Illegal Electric Fences: Unregulated direct-to-grid electrical connections set by farmers around cultivation plots have claimed giants like Revatha of Kala Wewa.
  • Jaw-Bombs (Hakka Patas): Crude improvised explosive bait left in pumpkin or vegetation that detonates in the elephant mouth, causing agonizing starvation.
  • Trap Guns and Poaching: High-powered rifle fire from organized ivory cartels, which claimed Galgamuwa Dala Poottuwa in 2017.
  • Train Collisions: Railway lines traversing critical migratory corridors across the North Central Province.

The Legend of Panamure and the End of Kraals

For centuries, wild elephants were captured through massive wooden enclosures called kraals. In August 1950, at the Panamure Kraal, a magnificent wild tusker refused capture and made a fierce stand to shield his trapped herd. When ordered to be shot by organizers, the tragedy provoked such unprecedented public outrage that Sri Lanka parliament banned elephant kraals forever. The Panamure tusker remains immortalized as a national emblem of freedom.

The Sacred Procession Tradition

Sri Lanka revered temple tuskers, such as the late National Treasure Nadungamuwa Raja and Millangoda Raja, carried the sacred Casket of the Buddha Relic at the Kandy Esala Perahera. Their calm intelligence, majestic stature, and bond with traditional mahouts created an indelible cultural bridge between nature and spirituality.
