Coexistence and Ecology

Shared Landscapes: The Path to Human-Elephant Coexistence

මිනිස්-අලි සහජීවනය උදෙසා එක්සත් මගක්

In the dry-zone frontier of Sri Lanka, wild elephants and rural farming villages share the exact same landscape. Achieving lasting peace requires understanding both sides with deep empathy, acknowledging the economic vulnerability of rural families while honoring the ecological needs of wild herds.

Understanding the Human Reality

Rural farmers in Galgamuwa, Maho, Anuradhapura, and Polonnaruwa represent the backbone of Sri Lanka's food security. Cultivating rice, corn, and vegetables under unpredictable rainfall, a family's entire annual income is invested into a single harvest.

When elephants cross into a cultivated field, a year of laborious toil can disappear within hours. Farming communities do not harbor malice toward wildlife; they are hardworking parents defending their children's sustenance and future against severe economic precarity.

Understanding the Elephant Reality

An adult Asian elephant requires up to one hundred and fifty kilograms of green fodder and two hundred liters of water every day. Over seventy percent of wild elephants in Sri Lanka live outside national park boundaries, roaming extensive home ranges governed by ancient generational memory.

When development schemes fragment contiguous forests, elephants do not distinguish between wild scrub and cultivated corn. They are intelligent, peaceful giants simply following the ancestral pathways etched into their lineage for millennia.

Community Managed Fences

Seasonal solar electric fences erected strictly around village perimeters and paddy fields during harvest, allowing elephants free movement through wilderness tracts during fallow months.

Protective Citrus Borders

Planting buffer crops like lime, orange, and ginger along forest boundaries, which elephants naturally avoid, shielding staple crops while creating secondary income for farmers.

Systemic Solutions Over Tragic Confrontation

Tragic losses like that of Kawanthissa and Galgamuwa Dala Poottuwa highlight that piecemeal barriers fail both farmers and wildlife.

Real coexistence requires transparent ecological planning, rapid farmer compensation funds, railway automated sensor slowdowns, and contiguous forest corridors connecting the tanks of the north-western basin.
