Operation Sentinel: Safeguarding Sri Lanka's 13 Endangered Wild Tuskers

How a joint task force combining military field units, aerial drone surveillance, and rural community coordination is charting a peaceful path for elephant conservation.

The Turning Point in National Wildlife Defense

In October 2026, the tragic loss of the iconic Galgamuwa giant Kawanthissa sent shockwaves across Sri Lanka. It brought into sharp focus the vulnerability of the island remaining wild tuskers, who constitute less than two percent of the total wild elephant population. Recognizing that existing wildlife ranger posts were stretched thin across vast forested terrains, the government initiated a historic response.

Under the leadership of Environment Minister Dr. Dhammika Patabendi and senior defense officials, Sri Lanka established a coordinated national task force. Rather than militarizing forest zones against local people, the initiative operates on a philosophy of protective guardianship, deploying technology and rapid response teams to protect both high-profile tuskers and the agricultural villages they roam near.

The 13 High-Priority Protected Giants

Wildlife scientists and regional Department of Wildlife Conservation (DWC) officers surveyed roaming patterns to pinpoint the 13 most vulnerable bulls traversing human-dominated landscapes. These elephants represent the living zenith of Asian elephant genetics:

  • Mahanama (Kumana & Arugam Bay): Navigating eastern coastal wetlands and lagoon fringes.
  • Kashyapa (Bundala & Weerawila): Grazing the saline Ramsar wetlands and crossing southern highways.
  • Wijayaba (Minneriya & Digampathaha): Traversing critical dry season transit corridors.
  • Suranimala (Kala Wewa): Sustaining the historic lineage of the Rajarata tank basin.
  • Agbo (Maha Kanadarawa & Thirappane): Under continuous compassionate rehabilitation and monitoring.
  • Sumedha of Udawalawe: The solitary mature sovereign of southern scrublands.
  • Sooryaya (Medawachchiya & Chilaw Circuit): The wide-ranging nomad crossing extensive transit corridors.
  • Mahasena (Lunugamvehera): The vital genetic anchor linking Yala with western river valleys.
  • Dighabhaya (Mahawilachchiya): Roaming the Wilpattu boundary buffer forests.
  • Gajabahu (Yan Oya, Wahalkada & Padaviya): The colossus of northeastern riverine woodlands.
  • Wanniya (Mullaitivu): A rare and prized tusker of the northern forests.
  • Maduru Oya Tusker: The quiet guardian of eastern reservoir rocky outcrops.
  • Walikanda Eastern Tusker: Navigating Mahaweli river floodplains and border farming belts.

Technology, Early Warning, and Community Respect

A central pillar of this operation is the establishment of a dedicated Operations Center at Army Headquarters linked in real time to DWC regional headquarters. Advanced thermal-imaging drones conduct nightly reconnaissance over known movement paths.

Crucially, this technology serves as an early warning system for rural farming communities. When an elephant approaches ripening paddy fields or village perimeters, farmers receive advance alerts, allowing rapid response teams to gently steer the animal back toward sanctuary reserves using safe illumination and sound deterrents. This preventative approach eliminates the desperation that leads to tragic traps, illegal electric fences, or explosive baits.

Preserving the Dignity of Both Farmers and Giants

True conservation succeeds only when rural people feel valued, heard, and supported. Smallholder farmers cultivate crops under demanding economic realities, and crop raids threaten their very livelihood. By offering 24-hour rapid security support, the state is standing beside rural cultivators, ensuring they do not bear the burden of national wildlife protection alone.

At the same time, the majestic tuskers, who carry immense cultural and ecological significance, gain a protective shield against poachers and accidental harm. Operation Sentinel stands as a beacon of hopeful, compassionate coexistence for Sri Lanka and the world.
