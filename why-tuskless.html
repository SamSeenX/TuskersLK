Evolutionary Biology & History

Why Do Only Two Percent of Sri Lankan Elephants Have Tusks?

ශ්‍රී ලංකා ඇතුන්ගේ දළ උරුමය සහ විකාශනය

Unlike African savanna elephants where both males and females carry long ivory tusks, Asian elephants exhibit a distinct biological pattern. In Sri Lanka, prominent ivory tusks are so rare that fewer than five percent of wild bulls carry them.

Tuskers vs Tuskless Bulls: Terminology in Ceylon

In Sri Lankan wildlife terminology, a male elephant with prominent ivory tusks is celebrated as a Dala Atha (Tusker). A male elephant without external tusks is known as an Aliya or Makhna. Female Asian elephants never grow full tusks, possessing only tiny internal or barely protruding incisors called Han-dala (tushes).

In southern India, over ninety percent of male Asian elephants carry tusks. Across the Palk Strait in Sri Lanka, the proportion drops to around two to five percent. This stark divergence has intrigued wildlife biologists and historians for centuries.

The Two Drivers: Natural Adaptation and Colonial Harvest

Evolutionary research points to a combined dynamic: an ancient island genetic trait amplified by intensive nineteenth-century human intervention.

1. Ancient Island Adaptation

During the Pleistocene epoch, ancestral herds on the island of Ceylon navigated thick rainforest canopies where massive ivory could hinder movement. Natural selection favored dense bone density and sheer body mass over energy-intensive tusk growth.

2. The 19th Century Trophy Pressure

During British colonial Ceylon in the nineteenth century, government bounties and sport expeditions systematically targeted tusked bulls for ivory export. This artificial removal of dominant tuskers created a genetic bottleneck, leaving tuskless bulls to pass on their lineage.

Protecting the Remaining Gene Pool

Because tusk-carrying genes are recessive and extremely scarce, every surviving wild tusker like Thirappane Agbo, Podi Dala Poottuwa, and Sumedha is a priceless biological treasure.

Protecting their migratory corridors and resolving agricultural edge conflicts peacefully is the only way to ensure future generations of Sri Lankans can still witness these majestic giants walking the tank beds.
